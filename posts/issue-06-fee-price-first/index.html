The Fee Isn’t the Problem. How You Say It Is.

Two of the most common reasons an answered call doesn’t book are the fee and the price question. How small HVAC and plumbing shops can say the fee once, clearly, and answer price shoppers with questions instead of a refusal.

The caller has no hot water. The CSR says the trip fee. There’s a pause, then “Okay, I’ll think about it.”

Or the caller asks, “How much to fix it?” The answer is “We don't quote over the phone.” The caller says thanks and dials the next shop.

Those are codes E and F from the Issue #4 reason log. Issue #5 was about calling them back. This issue is about the first call, so fewer of them end up on the callback list at all.

None of what follows is proven to book more calls. Nobody has published solid data on that. These are common practices from vendors and trade columns, and you can check them against your own Friday counts.

Write the fee down once

Before anyone says the fee on the phone, put three things on one card: the amount (yours, not anybody’s average), what it covers, and whether it comes off the repair bill. Our free Fee and No-Slot Call Scripts have a fill-in list for exactly this, including a yes/no blank for whether each fee is credited.

If you’d rather have it on paper, print our free Fee Card, a one-page sheet with those blanks and the phone lines, to keep by the phone.

Every person who answers says it the same way.

Jobber’s plumbing pricing guide (Apr 29, 2026) puts it plainly: “Customers don't mind paying, but they also don't like surprises.” Its advice is to say a fee applies before service, and to explain what it includes. ServiceTitan’s diagnostic-fee blog post (undated, accessed October 2026) says the same thing: “be upfront and honest about what they will be charged.”

Credited or not is your call. Jobber describes both setups: some plumbers keep the fee separate so it only covers the diagnosis, and others apply it to the repair if the customer approves the work. ServiceTitan’s Voice Agent objection-handling playbook (updated Oct 16, 2026) has wording for both, under “Fee waived with service” and “Fee not waived.” Nobody has published which one books more. Pick one, write it on the card, and stop changing it call by call.

Roofers: many roofing shops do free inspections or estimates. If you don’t charge a fee, skip this part and go to the price section.

Say it once, then offer a time

Here’s the order that keeps showing up in vendor guidance: say the fee, say what it covers, say what happens to it, then offer a time.

ServiceTitan’s objection-handling playbook has a sample line for the “Fee not waived” case. That page was written for its AI phone agent and is labeled as inspiration, so treat this as an example only:

“Our dispatch fee covers travel and the initial assessment so our technician arrives prepared. We'll give you a full written quote before any work begins — no surprises.”

The same page gives three tips that work for people too: “Acknowledge first”, “Keep it under 2 sentences.” and “Always end with a next step — offer a time or hold a slot.”

For a daytime call, that means: the tech diagnoses it and gives a written price before any work starts, here’s the fee and what it covers, here’s whether it comes off the bill, and here are two times. The fee section of the scripts has the daytime line, with the credited and not-credited sentences ready to pick from.

ServiceTitan’s post also gives a reason the fee helps the customer: it “protects them from paying an hourly rate on a diagnostic visit that runs especially long.” If that matches how you charge, it’s worth one sentence.

The same section has the after-hours version. The no-slot section covers what to say when there’s no slot.

When they push back on the fee

Don’t repeat the number louder.

ServiceTitan’s release notes for its AI agent’s objection handling (April 2026) describe the failure in one line: “The Voice Agent repeats the dispatch fee back to the caller.” The result in that scenario: “The homeowner calls a competitor.” That’s the vendor’s made-up example, not data, but most owners have heard a person do the same thing.

The practice instead: acknowledge it once, say what the fee covers in one sentence, and offer a time. That also follows the playbook’s tips. Our scripts put it this way:

“I understand. That fee gets a tech out and covers finding the problem, and you get a written price before any work starts. I have [time]. Want it?”

If they still say no, don’t argue. Offer your next real option, and if nothing books, circle E. The pushback line, and the one for when they still say no, are in the fee section of the scripts.

If you already use ServiceTitan’s Voice Agent (as of October 2026): its objection-handling settings let you add your own responses to fee pushback (release hub, April 2026; playbook updated Oct 16, 2026). The point isn’t the software. It’s that the answers get written down ahead of time. A card next to the phone does the same thing.

“How much?” Don’t open with a no

“We don't quote over the phone” is true for a lot of shops. As the whole answer, it’s a closed door.

Steve Coscia, writing in Contracting Business (undated archive, accessed October 2026), says callers who don’t get a quote “feel justified in calling another contractor until they get what they want.” His suggested line:

“That's a great question and we can give you a quote after we get visual confirmation about what is wrong. Can I schedule an appointment for you today?”

The ACCA HVAC Skill Builder Q&A (undated, accessed October 2026) makes the same point: the call taker should let the caller know “that you need to have technician come out and do a full and complete diagnosis.” The key is telling them why, not just no.

Tell them why you won’t guess. A plumbing column in PHCP Pros (Nov 4, 2022) lays it out: “You are untrustworthy if you quote a phone price that you can't stand behind and raise your price after seeing the job.” And: “You may actually be helping your competitor get the job instead of you.” Callers can understand that, so say it out loud.

Then the practice, in order: say yes to the question, ask a few quick questions (what it’s doing, what kind of system and about how old, when it started), then give what you can stand behind. On a repair call, that’s the fee and what it covers, never a repair range. Then offer a time.

The price-first section of the scripts has the questions and the lines word for word, for daytime and after-hours calls.

Replacement shoppers: a written range beats a refusal

Someone shopping for a new system is a different call. A refusal there sounds like you’re hiding something.

Scott Merritt of Fire & Ice, in a 2021 ACHR News column on “The Ballpark Estimate” question, says “refusing to provide one (or guessing) rarely works.” His practice comes down to a few questions, a rough range that’s wide on purpose, the caveats said plainly, and then an in-home visit.

That column is about install sales, not repair calls. For repairs, give the fee, never a repair range. And only give a replacement range if your shop has settled on one in writing. If it hasn’t, skip the range and offer the visit. A number someone made up on the call is the PHCP Pros problem all over again. The replacement-shopper script in the price-first section has the questions, the written-range-only line, and the no-range fallback.

Merritt’s tone note works for both kinds of call: “The worst thing we can do as contractors is to sound like we're annoyed by all of this.”

Log it and check Friday

Keep E and F on your Issue #4 reason card. Our suggestion: add a small check mark when the fee was said with what it covers (E), or when questions were asked and a fee or range was offered (F).

On Friday, compare E and F for the two weeks before you changed how you say it and the two weeks after. That’s just your shop’s count. It won’t prove anything for anyone else, and it might not move. If it doesn’t, the fee itself may be the question, and that’s an owner decision.

A note on fee rules

This is not legal advice.

You may have heard about the FTC’s rule on junk fees. Its fee rule (16 CFR Part 464) covers only “Live-event tickets; or... Short-term lodging”. It doesn’t set fee-disclosure rules for home-service calls.

State and local rules are a different story, and we didn’t research them. Before you change how you state or credit a fee, check with your state contractor board or a lawyer.

Soft close — one card, said the same way

The fee isn’t usually the hard part. Saying it three different ways on three different calls is. Write it down once: the amount, what it covers, and whether it comes off the bill. Answer “how much?” with a couple of questions before a no. Then offer a time.

A few things to try this week, if they fit:

  • Write your fee on one card with what it covers, and whether it’s credited. Tape it next to the reason card.
  • Strike “we don't quote over the phone” as a full answer. Start with “happy to help” and a couple of questions.
  • On Friday, count the E’s and F’s. Do it again in two weeks.
