“No Slot” Isn’t a Phone Problem. It’s a Schedule You Set on Friday.

When a caller can’t get a slot that works, the fix usually isn’t on the phone. Five things small HVAC, plumbing, electrical and roofing owners can change so fewer good calls end with “we’re booked.”

The caller is ready. No cooling, house is 84 degrees, they’ll take whatever the fee is. The CSR checks the board and there’s nothing until Thursday. The caller says thanks and calls the next shop.

That’s code G on the Issue #4 reason card: no slot that worked. The card says it plainly: it’s a capacity call, and that’s on the owner, not the phone.

The no-slot section of our call scripts already covers what your CSR says in that moment: don’t fake a hold, offer the next real option, and log the G. This issue is the other half. Whoever answers can’t create a slot that doesn’t exist. You can.

A straight warning up front: nobody we found has published solid data showing any of this books more calls. These are practices from vendor guides and trade columns, plus a couple of our own suggestions, labeled as ours. Check them against your own Friday count.

1. Hold some capacity open on purpose

If every slot on the board fills with tune-ups by Tuesday, the emergency caller on Wednesday hears “no.”

ServiceTitan’s help doc on capacity limits (Oct 2026) gives this example:

“A residential HVAC business reserves 25% of summer capacity for emergency no-cool calls by capping maintenance and tune-up job types at 75%.”

Its table suggests capping maintenance and tune-ups at 70–80%, which “Leaves 20–30% open for emergencies. Adjust by season.” The doc calls these numbers “a reasonable starting point.” That’s a vendor’s suggestion, not a measured result, so treat it as a place to start thinking, not a target.

The paper version (our suggestion, not ServiceTitan’s): block one slot per tech per day that routine work can’t take. If nobody urgent claims it by a set time, give it to the first name on your callback list (more on that below). You pick the time. We don’t know of a right answer, so choose one and stick with it for a few weeks. If you’d rather have it on paper, print our free Weekly Capacity Rules sheet, a one-page sheet where you write down your held slots, release time and callback owner once, and keep it by the schedule.

2. Move routine work, not the new emergency

When the day is full and an emergency comes in, something has to give. The question is what.

Butch Welsch of Welsch Heating & Cooling took this on in an old but on-point ACHR News column (Nov 23, 2005). A reader with 15 techs wrote that they hated to cancel maintenance calls, even if it meant turning away new customers with an emergency. Welsch’s answer was to “arrange the schedule so that we could handle a minimum number of emergency calls within our normal eight-hour days.” When the day overflowed, overtime picked it up: “Sometimes the overtime calls were the emergency calls, and sometimes the overtime calls were just the routine maintenance calls.”

The practice: the maintenance customer who booked two weeks ago can usually move a day with a polite call. The person with water on the floor can’t wait a day. If you move anything, move the routine work, and call those customers yourself instead of letting them find out at the door.

Welsch also had a line for the balance: “The trick is to have too many customers but make them feel like they are your only customer!”

3. Keep arrival windows you can actually hit

Every major field-service tool lets you set how wide your arrival windows are. As of October 2026:

  • Jobber offers default windows from “None” and 15 minutes up to 4 hours, and you can “Add window after start time” or “Center window after start time.”
  • Housecall Pro (help article dated Aug 6, 2026) has the same range, from none up to 4 hours.
  • ServiceTitan notes that “arrival windows can overlap one another.”

Our take, not a study: a wider window you keep beats a tight window you miss. We don’t have data that wider windows cut G calls, so we won’t claim it. But a two-hour promise that turns into four hours costs you a callback, an apology, and sometimes the next job’s slot too. If your windows keep slipping, that’s capacity information. Write it down.

4. Don’t let the website promise slots the phone wouldn’t

The scripts say no fake holds on the phone. Your online booking should follow the same rule.

If you use online booking, check what it’s allowed to offer:

  • Jobber has an “Earliest availability” setting. Per its help center, “setting this to 1 business day away prevents clients from booking same-day appointments.”
  • Housecall Pro says: “If you need to block off dates on your calendar to prevent customers from booking online, use Events.”
  • ServiceTitan’s capacity FAQ says that when a window is “100% full, the arrival window box is blank.”

The settings differ, but the rule is the same: if your held slot from step 1 is open to the website, a tune-up booking can grab it at 9 p.m. and the emergency caller still hears “no.” Make sure the website can only book what you’d book on the phone.

5. After hours, the on-call tech is for emergencies

ServiceTitan describes this setup: “A plumbing company restricts after-hours capacity to emergency job types only, so on-Call technicians are never assigned to non-urgent maintenance overnight.” That’s a vendor’s example, not a result, but the idea works on paper too. If routine work can land on the on-call schedule, your on-call tech is already busy when the real call comes in.

The paper version: one written rule, taped by whatever phone takes after-hours calls. After a set time, only the job types you’ve listed as emergencies go to on-call. Everything else gets the first real daytime slot.

6. Keep a callback list for G callers (our suggestion)

This one isn’t from a trade source. We looked and found only software vendors’ blogs on cancellation waitlists, so we’re not citing them. It’s our suggestion.

Keep a short list of no-slot callers: name, number, the problem, and the earliest time that works for them. When a cancellation opens a slot, or your held slot from step 1 isn’t claimed, call the top name first.

One rule: the list has to be real. Don’t tell a caller “we’ll call you if something opens” unless one named person owns the list and checks it. A callback promise nobody keeps is a fake hold with extra steps. If you track callbacks the way we covered in Issue #5, this list can live in the same place.

7. Have a referral partner you trust

Sometimes there’s no slot, no overtime, and no cancellation coming. Sending the caller to someone good is better than a dead end.

Anja Smith of All Clear Plumbing wrote about referrals in Plumber magazine (Oct 22, 2020): “When a customer calls, they just want their problem solved. Whether you do the job yourself or point them in the right direction, they will be grateful.”

To be clear, she was writing about work her shop doesn’t do. Using the same idea for “we can’t get to you soon enough” is our own extension. Her cautions carry over well, though:

  • “Who you recommend reflects on your business.” Don’t pick a name at random. If you don’t know anyone good, she says: “It may be better to say you don’t have anyone you are comfortable recommending.”
  • “I keep money out of the equation.” If a referral fee is what makes you recommend someone, the customer will eventually notice.

Pick one or two shops you’d send your own family to, and talk to them before you need them. Every referral is still a G on your card. Circle it.

Friday: count G by day of week

This is where you check whether any of the above is working.

On the reason card, G already sits in that day’s row, so you can see which weekday it lands on. On Friday, look at two things: which days the G’s land on, and what kind of job they were (emergency, repair, maintenance, install).

ServiceTitan’s doc suggests holding “more capacity for emergencies on Mondays (when demand spikes after weekends).” That’s the vendor’s claim, not data, and your shop may look nothing like it. Your card will tell you. The same doc has a good review question: “Days where you are consistently full early — consider raising caps or adding capacity.”

If one day keeps showing up, that day gets the held slot. If the G’s spread across every day of the week, the issue may not be how you schedule. You may need more people. Welsch said as much in 2005: when overtime got excessive, they knew they were in a position to hire another service technician.

Change one thing at a time. Compare two weeks before and two weeks after. That’s only your shop’s count. It won’t prove anything for anyone else, and it might not move.

If you already use scheduling software

As of October 2026, here’s where each of these lives:

  • ServiceTitan: job-type capacity caps (Adaptive Capacity is part of ServiceTitan Max and Scheduling Pro, so not every shop has it), after-hours rules by job type, and the capacity check that blanks out full arrival windows.
  • Jobber: arrival window defaults and the “Earliest availability” setting for online booking.
  • Housecall Pro: arrival window settings and Events to block dates from online booking.

The software doesn’t make the decision. You still decide how much to hold, what moves first, and who owns the callback list. A sheet of paper by the phone can hold the same rules.

Soft close — G is your number

Your CSR can say “no slot” kindly and honestly. The scripts handle that. Whether they have to say it as often is up to you.

A few things to try this week, if they fit:

  • Pick one weekday and hold one slot per tech that routine work can’t take.
  • Write down what moves first when an emergency comes in, and who calls the routine customer.
  • Start a G callback list with one named owner.
  • On Friday, count the G’s by weekday. Do it again in two weeks.
