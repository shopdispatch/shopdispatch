“Let Me Think About It” Is a Question. Ask It Back Once.

When a caller says “I’ll think about it,” there’s usually a real reason underneath. How small HVAC, plumbing, electrical and roofing shops can ask once, answer that reason, hold an honest slot, and know when to stop.

The caller has described the problem. Your CSR has given the fee, the way the call scripts say to. There’s a pause, and then: “Okay. Let me think about it and call you back.”

On the reason card that’s code H, calling back or thinking it over. It counts as bookable but lost. Issue #5 covered what happens after the call: call back the same business day, three tries, log it. This issue is about the thirty seconds before the caller hangs up.

A straight warning up front: we didn’t find any published data showing that a clarifying question, a held slot or a follow-up text books more of these calls. What follows is vendor guidance, a couple of old trade columns, and our own suggestions, labeled as ours. Check it against your own Friday count.

1. H is a label, not a reason

“I’ll think about it” sounds like one thing. It usually isn’t.

ServiceTitan’s objection playbook for its AI phone agent (as of October 2026) lists the same hesitation under several labels: “I’m not sure. Let me think about it.” and “I might call around first.” in one place, “I need to check with my husband/wife first” in another, “That seems like a lot” in another. The playbook says its scripts “serve purely as inspiration,” so treat them that way, not as data.

Our read, not a study: behind H there are usually about six stories. The fee worried them and they didn’t say so. They need to check a calendar. They’re shopping around. It isn’t urgent. Someone else has to say yes. Or they really just aren’t sure. Same words, six different calls. We don’t know how often each one happens, and nobody we found has published it.

2. Ask one honest question, once

Adams Hudson put the move plainly in a Contracting Business column back in 2005: “What exactly do you want to think over?” and “You must reduce this intangible to a specific objection.” He was writing about in-home sales, not the phone, so take the idea, not proof that it works on calls.

On the phone, a softer version works better. Our wording:

“Of course. Is there anything I can clear up now, like the fee, the timing, or someone else who needs to weigh in? If not, no problem.”

Naming the three likely reasons means the caller doesn’t have to invent one. Then take the answer. If they say “No, just thinking,” the CSR says “No problem” and moves to step 4 or 5. Don’t ask twice. Don’t ask “Why aren’t you ready?”

ServiceTitan’s playbook has a good rule for the tone: “Acknowledge first — “I understand” before pivoting always lands better. Keep it under 2 sentences.” And Nancy Friedman, writing in Contractor magazine in 2005: “Don’t tell people what they have to do.”

3. Answer only that reason. Don’t pitch.

Whatever they say, answer that and stop.

  • The fee. Use the fee line from the scripts page. Don’t re-explain it a new way. If they still say no, it’s an E, not an H.
  • The calendar. Offer a real slot they can hold (step 4).
  • Shopping around. One friendly line and no badmouthing. ServiceTitan’s playbook says it straight: “Never badmouth competitors.” Offer a held slot or a written summary. If they’re getting other quotes, that’s a J.
  • Not urgent. Offer a later slot. Don’t push. Angie Snow, a CSR coach quoted on ServiceTitan’s blog, makes the point that the caller’s answers tell you how urgent it is. The blog’s example: “If it’s something that’s been happening for two months, it’s not necessarily an urgent heating or air conditioning call.”
  • Someone else has to say yes. That’s its own code. See step 6.

If the answer changes the reason, change the code. That’s the point of asking.

4. If it fits, hold a real slot, and say how long

The tentative slot is the move vendors recommend most for “I’ll think about it.” ServiceTitan’s playbook offers it several ways, for example: “I can hold a tentative spot on the schedule so you’re not scrambling when you’re ready. There’s no commitment until you confirm.” That’s a script, not a result. Nobody has shown us it books more.

Here’s where we part ways with some of the advice out there. Snow’s CSRs say “our schedule is filling up” and, in her words, “My CSRs do not get to use the word ‘cancel.’” We’d do the opposite. Our rules for an honest hold:

  • Only hold a slot you checked. Same rule as the scripts page: no fake holds.
  • Don’t say “we’re filling up” unless you are.
  • Say how long you’ll hold it, out loud. “I’ll hold Thursday 8 to 10 until 4 today. After that it goes back on the board.”
  • Say plainly how to cancel. “If it doesn’t work, call or text this number and we’ll free it up.”
  • Decide who can place holds, and how many. A hold costs a slot. That’s the tradeoff from Issue #7. Write it on your Weekly Capacity Rules sheet next to your held emergency slots.

We didn’t research cancellation or no-show fees. If you don’t already have a written policy, leave them out of this.

5. Put it in writing right after the call, if they’re OK with it

Ask first: “Is it OK if we text or email you the details?” Write down the answer.

If yes, send a short summary within minutes. Whatever the CSR said on the phone goes in writing, in the same words. Our suggested contents:

  • The problem, in their words
  • The fee, what it covers, and whether it’s credited (from your written rules, same wording as the call)
  • The held window and when the hold ends, if there is one
  • How to book or cancel
  • One phone number

No price guesses, no promos, no “limited time.” It isn’t a quote unless you can actually quote.

Housecall Pro gives similar advice about sent quotes, which is close but not the same as a phone call: tell the customer “when they’ll hear from you and that you’re available for questions or changes,” and “Before you send a quote follow-up by text, make sure you have permission to text the customer.”

The paper version: one text template saved on the office phone, with blanks for the problem, window and hold time.

6. “I need to ask my wife/landlord/boss” is code I, not H

On the reason card, needing someone else’s yes already has its own code: I, needs spouse, owner or landlord. When the caller’s answer to your one question is a person, change H to I.

Then ask one more thing: “When will they be around?” Write it in the notes (“Thu evening, both home”). That keeps them off the H callback list. The night-call log already says I “isn’t on the callback list,” because it depends on when the decider is around. If the caller names a time, one call at that time is keeping a promise, not a callback campaign.

Our hunch, not data: asking ServiceTitan’s two required-information questions early, “Do you own the property? If not, who does?” and “Who is responsible for payment?”, may surface the landlord or the spouse before the fee comes up instead of after.

7. Stop on purpose

Issue #5 set three callback tries as the most we’d suggest. Here’s what ends it sooner, or changes it (our rules):

  • A clear no or “stop” ends it right away, on any channel. Log “Asked not to be contacted” and keep that row. Don’t delete it.
  • If they picked a date (“call me Friday”), call once on that date. That’s the follow-up. Friedman’s old advice applies: don’t say “as soon as possible,” give them a time, and keep it.
  • Otherwise, three tries, the same as Issue #5.
  • For long waits (spring, insurance, after the holidays), ask if it’s OK to reach out then, and put one dated reminder in the notes. Not a drip.
  • After the last try, log “No answer after all tries” and stop.

Housecall Pro’s quote advice lands in the same place: “Be persistent, not pushy. Two or three follow-ups are plenty.”

A short legal note (not legal advice)

The FCC’s new rules on how people opt out of calls and texts (FCC 26-67) were published in the Federal Register on Oct 26, 2026, and take effect Nov 25, 2026. We haven’t read the full order and won’t summarize beyond that. For a small shop, three plain habits cover most of it: ask before you text, keep the text about their call, and treat any clear “stop” as final. Rules differ by state. Ask your lawyer before you text customers.

Friday: find out what H actually is

If H is your biggest code, the first fix isn’t more callbacks. It’s finding out what’s inside H.

Ten minutes on Friday, from the log:

  1. How many H’s this week, and what share of bookable-but-lost they are.
  2. For each H still standing, the one-word reason in notes: calendar, not urgent, or unsure.
  3. How many H’s turned into another code on the call after the one question: E, F, I or J. That’s the question working.
  4. How many holds you placed, and how many were kept, cancelled, or released at the hold time.
  5. How many H’s got a written summary.

One rule: count a held slot as booked only if the slot was checked, the same rule the night-call log already uses. Put kept-versus-cancelled holds right next to it, so your booking rate can’t quietly improve on holds that never show up. And keep H in the booking-rate math. Moving it to the excused pile would just flatter the number.

Change one thing at a time. Compare two weeks before and two weeks after. That’s your shop’s count, not proof for anyone else, and it might not move.

If you already use scheduling software

As of November 2026, a few tools have pieces of this. They’re examples of writing it down, not recommendations:

  • ServiceTitan: the Voice Agent objection playbook (objections 3 to 6 are closest to this issue) and, for roofing, a Follow Up module that dates follow-ups by reason. Its own pages don’t agree on whether a call-back caller counts as a lead, which is a good reminder that you decide once and stick with it.
  • Jobber: booking confirmations and reminders. A confirmation needs a scheduled visit or assessment first, so a hold has to be on the schedule before you can confirm it. Text confirmations are on select plans and only in the US, UK and Canada.
  • Housecall Pro: Pipeline stages, covered in Issue #5.

A text template on the office phone and a notes column on the log do the same job.

Soft close: one question, one hold, one text

“I’ll think about it” isn’t a no. It isn’t a yes either. It’s a question your CSR can ask back, once, kindly.

A few things to try this week, if they fit:

  • Put the one-question line by the phone.
  • Write down who can place a hold, how many, and for how long.
  • Save one summary text template on the office phone.
  • On Friday, split your H’s by reason.
