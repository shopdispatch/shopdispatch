Issue #9 · November 16, 2026

Who Else Has to Say Yes? Get a Name and a Time.

When a caller says “I have to ask my wife” or “I have to ask my landlord,” the call isn’t over. How small HVAC, plumbing, electrical and roofing shops can find out who decides, help the caller reach them, and on a rental, ask who authorizes and who pays before booking.

The caller has described the problem and heard the fee. Then: “I need to check with my husband first.” Or: “It’s a rental. I’d have to ask my landlord.”

Issue #8 said that answer isn’t code H. On the reason card it’s code I, needs spouse, owner or landlord, and the CSR asks one more thing: “When will they be around?” This issue picks up from there. The caller has told you someone else decides. What happens in the next minute, and what changes when that someone is a landlord instead of a spouse?

A straight warning up front: we didn’t find any published data on how often this happens, or showing that a three-way call, a forwarded summary or an early “do you own the home?” question books more of these calls. What follows is vendor guidance, a few trade-press articles about in-home replacement sales (not phone service calls), two government sources, and our own suggestions, labeled as ours.

1. Decide once whether I counts. We’d keep it.

Software vendors don’t agree on whether “I have to ask my landlord” is a lead.

  • ServiceTitan (as of November 2026) treats a call as a lead unless its call reason says otherwise, and an “Is Lead” call is one that “should have been converted into a job.” ServiceTitan’s write-up of a podcast with Sydney Biancardi of Iceberg Home Services puts it this way, in the blog’s own words rather than a direct quote from her: “Calls where a customer didn’t book because of a dispatch fee or needed to check with a spouse? Still a lead, even though no job was booked.”
  • Jobber (as of November 2026) keeps a contact labeled a lead until a job is created, an invoice is sent or a quote is approved. A caller waiting on a decider is simply still a lead.
  • Service Direct, a company that sells pay-per-call leads, says in a 2023 guide that it “refunds a call from tenants.” That’s a billing policy, and it points the other way.

Our position: keep I in bookable but lost, the way the card and the night-call log already do. Moving tenant calls to the excused pile would just flatter the booking rate. The one exception is a shop with a written rule that it doesn’t take work orders from tenants at all. That’s closer to D, a job type you don’t take. Either way, the owner writes the rule down once and the CSR never argues it call by call. Same idea as Issue #4.

2. “Someone else decides” is two different calls

A spouse who’s at work and a landlord who pays are not the same problem. We’d split I in two (our split, not a vendor’s):

  • Household I: spouse or partner, parent, adult child. The caller can usually reach them today, and the caller’s household is paying.
  • Property I: landlord, property manager, or an owner who isn’t the caller. Someone else may have to authorize the work, and someone else may pay.

ServiceTitan’s Contractor Playbook lists questions it says “should be asked on every call,” including “primary and additional contacts for the account” next to the two Issue #8 quoted about ownership and payment. Building on that, our wording for one early question:

“Is this your home, or do you rent? And is anyone else part of the decision on a repair like this?”

That’s our suggestion. We have no data that it changes outcomes. If the answer is a person, the CSR says “No problem. Who, and when are they around?”, codes I, and writes down one word for who decides: spouse/partner, family, landlord/PM, or other owner.

3. Household I: help the caller reach the decider

Start by separating the visit from the decision. On a no-heat call or a leak, the caller can usually say yes to a diagnostic visit. A contractor quoted by ACHR News in 2015, talking about no-heat and no-cool replacement calls, put it this way: “Something needs to be done right away. We schedule the appointment based on the customer’s convenience, so it doesn’t matter if there’s a missing homeowner.” The repair or replacement decision is where the other person comes in.

When the decider does matter, offer the caller ways to reach them. Our suggested order:

  1. Get them on the line now, if it’s easy for the caller. A three-way call or a speakerphone. Service Direct’s guide, written about tenants, says it plainly: “Pretend it’s 1995 and do a three way phone call with the tenant and property owner.” The same ACHR News article describes one HVAC company whose salespeople, in the home, ask if an absent spouse “can become available to hear a portion of the conversation,” by phone, email, text or FaceTime. That was in-home replacement sales, but the move carries over.
  2. Hold a real slot until after the decider’s time. Use Issue #8’s honest-hold rules: only a slot you checked, say how long you’ll hold it, say how to cancel. Set the hold to end after the decider gets home, not before.
  3. Send one summary the caller can forward. ServiceTitan’s objection playbook for its AI phone agent (as of November 2026) offers: “I can also have my team followup with our details so you have everything handy for that conversation.” Use Issue #8’s summary contents, and ask the caller first.
  4. Book a time when both are home, only if the job needs it. Mostly replacement estimates.

Don’t make “both of you home” a condition. The same ACHR News piece paraphrases a St. Louis contractor as saying that requiring both immediately makes the homeowner wary and distrustful that he or she will be in for a strong-arm type of sales pitch. Pro Remodeler (2016, home-improvement sales) adds: “And whatever you do in setting the appointment, avoid leaving homeowners feeling pressured.” A gentle invitation works better than a rule. HVAC Today (2018, replacement sales appointments) suggests: “Is there anyone else you might want to invite?”

4. Property I: who says yes and who pays come before the slot

On a rental, the first booking question isn’t “when.” It’s “who says yes, and who pays.”

Service Direct’s guide makes the point for call takers: “Your company, or even state laws, can dictate who you can accept a work order from. Wherever that line lies, it must be crystal clear to your call takers.” It also warns against the opposite extreme, a company that hung up the moment it heard “renter”: “it doesn’t serve the caller.”

Our suggestion, before you book anything beyond what the caller is paying for, is to get three things:

  • Who authorizes the work, by name and phone, or the property manager’s maintenance line.
  • Who pays the trip or diagnostic fee, and who pays the repair.
  • Where the yes is written down: a text or email from the landlord or manager, or their own work order.

If the tenant wants to go ahead and pay for the visit themselves, say plainly that it’s their bill and the fee comes first. Don’t take a tenant’s yes on a replacement or a big repair.

Tenants may have signed something that says the same. One property manager’s tenant handbook (Richey Property Management, Virginia, 2014) tells residents to “get an agreement and authorization in writing, in specific detail, prior to beginning work.” That’s one company’s handbook, not a rule, but it’s a fair picture of what your caller may be up against.

And the safety exception: a gas smell, active flooding or an electrical fire risk gets escalated under your scripts’ rules. “It’s a rental” never slows that down.

Rentals: a short note (not legal advice) > Before you book a slot on a rental, ask who authorizes the work and who pays. A tenant’s verbal OK isn’t the owner’s authorization. Rules on repairs and who can order them vary by state and by lease. Two examples: the Georgia Attorney General’s consumer page on landlord repairs, and California’s landlord-repair rules. They’re examples, not a summary of your state. Check your own state’s rules and ask your lawyer.

5. Three don’ts

  • Don’t question their right to decide. Some sales training still teaches a comeback along the lines of “I thought you could make your own decision?” Leave it out. Wanting to ask a spouse or a landlord is a normal thing to do, not an objection to beat.
  • Don’t invent a deadline. The Iowa Attorney General tells homeowners to “Be cautious of ‘today only’ prices, urgent claims, or pressure to sign before you can compare options.” Our rule: don’t say the decider has to answer “by tonight” unless a real, checked slot is being held until then. If there’s a safety issue, say what it is and escalate it. Don’t use it as a closing tool.
  • Don’t take a tenant’s yes for the landlord’s. Software makes this easy to miss. Jobber (as of November 2026), for example, lets the office mark a quote approved “for those times where your client agrees verbally with the outlined work.” A status in the software isn’t authorization. On a rental, the approval should come from the landlord or manager, or be backed by their written yes.

One more habit (ours, not researched as law): the decider never gave you their number. A three-way call the caller sets up, or a summary the caller forwards, keeps the caller in control. If you’ll call or text the landlord or spouse directly, ask the caller first (“Is it OK if we call them at that number?”), and treat any “stop” from that person as final. The opt-out habits from Issue #8’s legal note apply to them too.

6. On the log: a name and a time get one call

The night-call log says I isn’t on the callback list. Issue #8 said that if the caller names a time, one call at that time is keeping a promise. The example sheet in Issue #4 already shows an I row (“Have to ask my landlord.”) with “Office calls Thu 10 a” as the next step. Here’s how those fit together (our rule):

  • I with a named person and a named time gets one call at that time. Log it.
  • I with no time stays off the callback list. No chasing.

If you want it in one place, the night-call log now has four `decider_next` columns. They’re our own suggestion, not a vendor feature:

  • Who decides (`decider_next_who`): spouse/partner, family, landlord/PM, or other owner.
  • Next step (`decider_next_step`), in a few words: “caller relays,” “3-way done on call,” “tenant texts PM, PM calls us,” “summary forwarded.”
  • Agreed time (`decider_next_time`), or blank if there isn’t one.
  • Called (`decider_next_called`): Y once you’ve made the one call at that time.

A blank time means nobody calls. A plain notes column does the same job if you’d rather keep it simple.

Friday: what’s inside your I’s

Ten minutes on Friday, from the log:

  1. How many I’s this week.
  2. How many were household and how many were property.
  3. Of the I’s with a named time, how many got their one call at that time.
  4. How many I’s became booked, or turned out to be another code.

Keep I in the booking-rate math. Change one thing at a time, compare two weeks before and two weeks after, and treat it as your shop’s count, not proof for anyone else. It might not move.

If you already use scheduling software

As of November 2026, a few tools have places to write this down. They’re examples, not recommendations:

  • ServiceTitan: Bill To can be changed on a job, which its help page says is for “instances where property owners or property management companies are paying for the cost of the job or project performed at the tenant’s location.”
  • Jobber: additional contacts, for when “a client may want a spouse or a tenant to be included in communications,” and property contacts for property managers.
  • Housecall Pro: sub-customers, for when “a tenant might schedule a job, but the homeowner is responsible for the payment,” and estimates that can be emailed to more than one address.

Soft close: a name and a time

“I have to ask” isn’t a no. It’s the caller telling you who else is in the room. Find out who, and when they’re around, and make it easy to reach them.

A few things to try this week, if they fit:

  • Put the early question (“Is this your home, or do you rent?”) by the phone.
  • Write down your shop’s rental rule once: who has to authorize, and who pays the fee.
  • Split Friday’s I’s into household and property.
