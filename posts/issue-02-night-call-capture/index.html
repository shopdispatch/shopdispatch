What the Person Who Takes the Night Call Has to Write Down Before Morning

Answering the night call is not the same as capturing it. The short packet a spouse, on-call tech, or answering service must leave so the shop can dispatch, state its own fee, or refuse without calling the homeowner back.

The phone on the nightstand did its job. Somebody answered. By 7 a.m. the note says “Mrs. Allen, no heat, call her,” and the first hour of the day is a second interview of a homeowner who already told the story once.

That is not a staffing problem. It is a blank card. The shop still does not know the service address, whether the main is off, whether anyone heard the trip fee, or whose name is on the call. Trade associations do not publish a rate for how often night notes come in incomplete, so this is not a “studies show” post. It is the minimum packet that lets a morning person dispatch, quote the shop’s own fee, or say no, without starting over.

Whoever holds the phone counts: the owner, the on-call tech, a spouse, a notebook, a live answering service, or an AI receptionist. The card has to work for all of them. Software is optional.

Answered is not captured

ServiceTitan’s contractor playbook calls a job booked to the wrong address one of the most preventable costs in a home-service business. It does not put a dollar on that sentence, and neither will this post. The failure mode is ordinary. The truck goes to the billing ZIP, or to a street someone half-heard at 11:40 p.m.

Al Levi’s 2009 clipboard, written for small plumbing, HVAC, roofing, and electrical shops, was already the test: name, address, phones, email, what the call is about, and the day and time slot you actually promised. If the night note cannot fill those lines, a new tool is not the fix.

A live answering service will follow the script you hand it. Ruby’s HVAC and plumbing pages sell 24/7 receptionists, emergency escalation, and customized scripts. They do not publish the fields. “They answered” is not a morning packet.

A note is done when someone who was not on the call can answer six questions from the page alone:

  1. Who do I call, at what number?
  2. Where is the house — the service address, not the billing address?
  3. What did they say is wrong, and is anything still unsafe or still leaking?
  4. Did we promise a window, a morning call, or a no?
  5. Did they hear our fee, and did they accept it?
  6. Whose name is already on this?

If any line is blank, the call was answered and not captured.

The night card

Write the symptom in the caller’s words and a shop bucket (“no heat,” “active leak”). Do not let the bucket erase the words. Do not diagnose, and do not quote a repair nobody has seen. Maurice Maio, writing in Plumbing & Mechanical in 2003, told CSRs to state a diagnostic or evaluation price and get the address, not to invent a firm repair total over the phone. Housecall Pro’s dispatcher template splits the same way: confirm the dispatch fee, and let the tech price the repair after they look. Model numbers, “how did you hear about us,” and a membership pitch can wait. A scared caller does not need a source code at midnight.

Who, and how we reach them. First and last name. One callback number, read back, especially if it is not the number that rang in. Caller ID is often a spouse’s phone. If the caller cannot approve the work, write that, and a name for the owner or property manager if they gave one. Fail the packet if the only plan is “they’ll call us.”

Service address, read back. Street, city, and a cross-check (ZIP, or “blue porch on the corner”). “Same as last time” is not good enough unless the account was opened and that address was read back. A billing ZIP and a first name is a failed note.

What they said. Trade: HVAC, plumbing, electrical, roofing, or not us. Their sentence, how long it has been going on, and the room or fixture if they know it. If the taker guessed a cause, mark it “guess, not confirmed.”

What is already shut off, and what is still unsafe. Facts only. See the short safety script below. Gas and carbon monoxide stop the long form.

The window they asked for, and the window the shop agreed. “Needs someone tonight,” “first call in the morning is fine,” or “this week is fine,” in their words. Then what you actually said: “We will call by 7:30 a.m. with a window,” or “on-call was texted, no arrival time given.” “Urgent” with no clock is not a promise. “We’ll be right there” when the on-call sheet is empty is a lie.

Access, if a truck might roll. Someone home, and when. Gate, pets, parking, where the equipment is. “Dog in the yard, gate code on the note, condenser on the north side” is enough. Wi-Fi passwords are not worth a second call.

The fee, from your sheet, and what they did with it. Name the shop’s own label: after-hours, dispatch, or diagnostic. Write the amount from the sheet, or write “fee not on the sheet — I did not make one up.” Then their response: heard and OK, heard and declined, asked to think, or never stated. “A fee may apply,” with no number, is not a yes. ServiceTitan’s voice-agent settings let a shop say a specific amount, mention that a fee may apply without naming it, or say nothing. The log has to record which of those three happened. If they declined, the outcome cannot be “booked.”

Membership is a should, not a must, unless the shop’s written rule changes the night fee for members. “Says they’re on the club, not verified” is enough. Do not hold the note hostage for an agreement number.

A filled card, kitchen leak. “Dana Ruiz, callback 555-0148, read back, different from the number that rang in. 418 West Pine, Springfield, blue porch, read back. Plumbing. ‘Water coming through the kitchen ceiling, started about an hour ago.’ Main is off. She wants someone tonight. Told her we have no night plumber; we will call by 7:30 a.m. with a morning window. After-hours fee not offered because we are not rolling. Message-only. Clipboard for the owner. Nobody woken.”

The bad card. “Mrs. Allen, no heat, call her.”

Safety is a short script, then you stop talking

A long intake during a gas smell is a bad capture, not a thorough one.

Gas odor. American Gas Association guidance: leave with everyone else, do not flip switches or use a phone in the area, and call 911 or the gas utility from outside. The night line is short on purpose. Still inside or already out? 911 or the utility called from outside? Callback number from outside? No truck until the building is cleared. Do not keep them on a kitchen interview.

Carbon monoxide alarm, or symptoms they think are CO. U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission: do not hunt for the source, go outside to fresh air, call 911, and do not re-enter until responders say so. Write “CO alarm, family outside, 911 called, we did not book a diagnostic tonight.” A furnace visit is the follow-up, after the house is released, or if the alarm returns within 24 hours and they want a qualified tech.

Burning smell, buzzing, a hot device, smoke, or sparks. Electrical Safety Foundation International lists those as reasons to hire a qualified electrician. Write smell, smoke, or sparks yes or no; whether 911 was called; whole house or one room; panel or appliance if they know. Smoke or flames is not a slot on the board. It is refused until the fire department clears it. Do not walk them through pulling a meter or a main unless that sentence is already on the shop’s own sheet. This post will not invent one.

Active water. Still flowing? Main off, only a fixture off, or they cannot find the main? Which room? Housecall Pro’s dispatcher script uses main-water shutoff as a safety confirm. ServiceTitan’s pipe-leak questions ask the same thing. “Kitchen ceiling, still dripping, main is off” is a usable line. “Main location unknown — still running” is also usable. Silence is not.

Roof water near power. IKO flags water on a fuse box as an emergency even in a small volume. If the leak is over a panel or a ceiling light, say so, and flag electrical on the same note.

Vulnerable people in the house (an infant, someone on medical equipment) are shop policy, not an association required field. One line is enough: “baby in the house,” or “no one at risk named.”

Three endings: booked, message-only, or refused

“We’ll try to get someone out” is a fourth outcome. It creates a false hold. There are three honest ones.

Booked means a window the on-call sheet can actually keep, in the right trade, repeated back with the address and the job type. ServiceTitan’s closing script is that restatement: name, address, day, time, job type. The paper version of “do not let the bot book into fantasy capacity” is the on-call list. If the name is not on that list for this trade tonight, it is not booked.

Message-only means no slot was promised, and a clock time for the callback was. Jobber’s receptionist, when it only takes a message, creates a task for people the shop names, dated the next day if the call was after hours. Housecall Pro’s after-hours modes are call you, text you, or log a private note. A private note nobody will read before the homeowner calls the next shop is not message-only. It is a blank. Typical reasons, one phrase: no on-call for that trade; caller chose morning; fee not decided; needs a person to price.

Refused means you said no, with a reason the morning owner can defend. Out of area. Not our trade. Gas, CO, or fire not cleared. Unsafe access. No capacity, and the shop will not pretend. They wanted a repair price you will not give blind.

ServiceTitan treats a call over 60 seconds with no job as something that needs a call reason, for coaching. That is a product rule for its users, not a claim that most real leads last 60 seconds. A notebook needs the same column or the owner cannot coach the night. Reason codes worth keeping short: fee declined, no capacity, wrong trade, out of area, safety hold, caller chose morning, hung up, spam, could not confirm address.

If an answering service or an AI already picks up, the change to ask for is this column. Jobber can also pause at a conversation cap and stop booking. The owner should not assume the line is still booking after that switch flips.

The lines that change the truck

These sit on top of the card. They are not a second interview. ServiceTitan’s job-type question bank is the daytime depth chart. Steal the safety and dispatch lines. Leave system age and the membership pitch for later, or the spouse is still on the phone at midnight.

HVAC. No heat, no cool, not keeping up, water from the unit, or a smell. Gas and CO use the short script and stop. If it is no heat, fuel matters: natural gas, propane, or electric. That changes the safety note and which tech. Indoor temperature if they know it. “Thermostat is blank” or “house feels cold, they didn’t read a number” is good enough. System type if they know it. Do not stall on model and serial.

Plumbing. Active leak, slow leak, no hot water, drain, or sewage. Active water and sewage outrank no hot water for a night truck. Main shutoff: off, not off, or they cannot find it. Room and floor, and whether it is still reaching finished space.

Electrical. Burning, smoke, or sparks, and whether 911 was called. One room, one circuit, or the whole house. “Smell is at the panel in the garage” is enough. Neighborhood-outage and breaker checks are optional, and only if they can do them without opening a burning panel.

Roofing. Is water coming into the house right now, or is it a stain, missing shingles, or “next rain”? IKO’s split: open to weather, structural risk, or water on electrical is a now problem. A couple of broken shingles or hail is a business-hours visit. Ask whether a tarp is already on, and who put it there. Record buckets inside if they have them. Do not tell anyone to climb or tarp the roof. IKO tells homeowners not to. The night taker writes down what already happened.

Name the human before you hang up

“On call” is not a name. ServiceTitan, Jobber, and Housecall Pro all make the shop type a forwarding number or pick an escalation contact before software will wake anyone. A notebook should do the same. ACCA’s after-hours questions are the same three decisions: who answered, who is actually available to fix it, and what they will walk into.

Write the person’s name, or write “no one was woken — morning owner owns it.” Write how they were told: live transfer, text at a clock time, clipboard, or the unbooked email list. Write what they already have. “Has the address and a yes on the fee” is different from “only has ‘no heat, call them.’” If the packet is still in one person’s head, say so. Fail the note if it says “dispatched” and no person is named.

The morning log is the product

Spouse, on-call tech, answering service, or software: same card. The shop gets faster when the blanks are visible. Fee declined. No slot. Wrong trade. Nobody woken. A dashboard that says “calls answered” hides all of that.

Before the next no-heat or active-leak night, write the lines on a card and put it by the phone the spouse actually answers. Morning should not start with “what was the address again?”

If someone else already picks up, hand them this list and ask for one change. Every night note ends booked, message-only, or refused, with a person’s name on it.

The fee on that card has to be yours. Say it, write down whether they accepted it, and do not send a truck on a blank. That is the whole system. The software can come later, or not.
